Plan 15, start to finish.

One feature end to end, with the numbers the tools reported: the mockups, the build, what the reviewers found, and what none of the agents could check.

Plan 15 was a redesign of Atrium's terminal app, plus a way to stop a running task. The terminal app is built with Ink, a library that lets you write terminal interfaces in React. It went from plan to merge today.

It went through the pipeline I described in Why one agent isn't enough: a plan, one builder in its own worktree, a fresh reviewer, a fix wave, a scoped re-review, every test group, then me. Here's how each step went, with the real numbers.

Two mockups, one pick

Before Plan 15 had a builder, it had mockups. For interface work the human decision moves earlier: I'd rather pick between pictures than describe a layout in words.

An agent drew two directions for the terminal app, A and B, as static frames at a real terminal size. I picked A, “Home with tabs”: tabs across the top, the things that need me as cards, what's running underneath, and an Ask box that's always there. Choosing between two concrete pictures took me a few minutes. Describing what I wanted would have taken longer and come out less accurate.

The mockup became the target and the plan became the contract. One ruling in the plan shaped everything after: screens are composed as fixed-width lines of text, not with Ink's flexbox layout. That way box edges line up exactly, 80×24 is laid out on purpose instead of squeezed, and mouse clicks are looked up in the same regions the screen drew. It also means every screen can be tested as text, without a terminal.

The builder ran for about 108 minutes and made 270 tool calls: reading files, editing them, running tests. It used about 650,000 tokens and finished with 7 commits across roughly 115 files.

That token count is a total over the whole run, not what it held at any one moment. A token is a chunk of text of about four characters, and an agent re-reads its whole conversation on every step, so most of a long run's tokens are the same context being read again. I wrote about why in Tokens, explained simply.

The review

Then a fresh reviewer read the branch. It used about 189,000 tokens over about 16 minutes, and found no critical issues, 2 important ones and 8 minor ones. The two important ones were both bugs I would have shipped.

  • Important Scrolling the mouse wheel typed letters into the Ask box.

    Terminals don't send mouse events as events. They send them as escape sequences, short strings of characters mixed in with your keystrokes, and the app has to pick them back out. The filter decoded them fine, but while the Ask box was open, the wheel's scroll keys were still handed to the box as typing, and clicking a card's key label typed that letter instead of pressing it. The fix: ignore the wheel while you're typing, and close the box on any click outside it.

  • Important Stop said it worked when it hadn't.

    If the task had already finished, or the agent ignored the stop signal, the app still reported that the task was stopped. For an app whose whole premise is telling me the truth about what agents did, that's the worst kind of bug. Now Stop reports what actually happened. I wrote that up in Stopping a running agent safely.

  • Found in the fix wave A “flaky” test was a real race.

    The reviewer saw one terminal-app test fail under load and pass on its own. The fix wave dug in and found a real race between two connections that could show a line of agent output twice. That one gets its own note: Testing code that agents write.

Neither of the first two showed up in the tests, because the tests checked what the builder thought to check. Neither was in the builder's report, because the builder believed both worked. The fix wave took about 78,000 tokens and fixed every finding. None was rejected.

The second review

A second fresh reviewer then looked at only the fix wave's changes, checked against the list of findings. It used about 114,000 tokens. Nine findings were fixed and one only partly, and it found one new important issue and two minor ones.

  • Important The money summary still carried real transactions.

    One of the minor fixes made the terminal app read a slimmed-down money summary, so no transaction rows reach it at all. But the monthly recap inside that summary still listed up to 3 real “notable” transactions a month, like the largest charge. The app never drew them, so nothing reached the screen. The data was in the process anyway, which is what the fix was meant to stop. It's stripped now.

  • Minor Two stops at once could disagree.

    If the dashboard and the terminal app stopped the same task at the same moment, the second could be told the task “finished before it could be stopped”, when it had in fact been stopped. Both now get the same answer.

  • Minor Esc followed quickly by a key could merge into one keypress.

    The mouse filter holds a lone Esc for 25 ms in case it's the start of a split mouse report. A key typed inside that window was joined to it. Now a held Esc is only joined to what follows when that could finish a mouse report.

All three were fixed with tests before I merged. The money one is my favorite example of why the re-review exists: the fix wave's own test passed because its sample data had no notable charge in it. It tested the fix, just not the case the fix was for.

What it cost

The first review cost under a third of the build, and it caught two bugs that the tests and the builder both missed.

I'll take that trade. The alternative was finding the Stop bug a week later, in a moment when I was trusting the app to tell me the truth.

What no agent could check

The builder couldn't see. It had no display. It rendered every screen at 80×24 and 120×36 and saved the frames as text, and it ran the app in a pseudo-terminal to check real clicks and raw bytes. That caught real problems. But it couldn't know how the app feels in Ghostty with a real font, a real mouse and a window being resized. The wheel bug was caught by a reviewer reading code and confirming it in a test harness, not by anyone scrolling. A human still has to sit with it in a real terminal, and for now that's me.

Spinners cost CPU. Each spinner frame is a full Ink redraw, about 40 to 60 ms of CPU. At four frames a second that was 25 to 30% of a core. The spinner now ticks once a second, only on the tabs that show it, and only while a task runs: about 8% of a core on Home while something runs, 0.8% on other tabs and 0.3% idle. Going lower means dropping the spinner or replacing Ink's renderer, and that's a decision I haven't made.

Voice comes last. I'd like to talk to Atrium from across the room. It's planned, but it goes after everything else, because a voice interface on top of a shaky core just gets you to the shaky parts faster.
